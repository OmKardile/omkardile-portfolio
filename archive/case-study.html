TL;DR

Amazon's filters are broken (sort mixed with filters, no price range, can't exit without applying), product pages are infinitely scrollable with no navigation, and design is inconsistent across surfaces. I redesigned the shopping experience with 5 research-backed fixes: sort separated from filters, price range inputs, tabbed product detail (Overview / Specs / Reviews), consistent card design, and accurate result count after filtering.

01 — Context
What it is

Amazon is the world's largest e-commerce platform with 2B+ monthly visitors. Despite its scale, the shopping UX has well-documented problems that frustrate users daily. This redesign focuses on the product search and detail experience — not the checkout flow.

02 — Problem
One sharp problem

Amazon's filter system is fundamentally broken: sort is buried inside filters instead of being separate, price range can't be specified, and users can't exit the filter panel without applying changes. Product pages are so long that users can't find specs or reviews without endless scrolling.

The core problem isn't visual — it's structural. Filters, sort, and product information are organized in ways that contradict standard e-commerce patterns.
03 — Constraints
What I worked within

Three days. Solo. No access to Amazon's real catalog or backend. All product data is fictional but the interaction patterns mirror real usage. The redesign had to work on desktop (1440px) and mobile (375px) without separate codebases.

04 — Process
Research → decisions

I started with a UX heuristic evaluation (Nielsen's 10 heuristics) sourced from Uptech's Amazon Shopping App UX Case Study, plus real user complaints from Reddit and LinkedIn. Five themes emerged:

  • Sort inside filters: "Strange to see sorting inside filters. This is usually a separate feature" (Uptech heuristic evaluation)
  • No price range: "Users can't choose the price range that they need" (Uptech)
  • Can't exit filters: "Can't get out of filters without applying them, and there's no exit button" (Uptech)
  • Product page too long: "Very long scrolling of the product page. Difficult to navigate and search for the necessary information" (Uptech)
  • Result count inconsistency: Filtering changes result count unpredictably — "Where did the other 1,600 go?" (LinkedIn: Raghav Bansal)
    • 05 — Key Decisions
    I chose X over Y because Z
    Why separate sort from filters?
    Because the research explicitly flagged "strange to see sorting inside filters" as a consistency violation. Standard e-commerce pattern: sort is a dropdown at the top of results, filters are in a sidebar. Mixing them violates user expectation. Sort changes order; filters change what's included. Different actions, different controls.
    Why price range inputs instead of checkboxes?
    Because the research found "users can't choose the price range that they need (can't choose a price less than $30)." Checkboxes force predefined ranges. Number inputs let users specify exact min/max. More flexible, more predictable.
    Why tabbed product detail instead of infinite scroll?
    Because the research found "very long scrolling, difficult to navigate." Tabs (Overview / Specs / Reviews) let users jump to what they need without scrolling. Each tab is short and scannable. Standard pattern on competing sites (Best Buy, B&H).
    Why show result count in the results header?
    Because the research found result count changes unpredictably after filtering. Showing "6 results" at the top — accurate after filters — builds trust. The count is always correct because it's derived from the actual filtered list, not a separate counter.
    Why a "Clear all" button always visible in the filter sidebar?
    Because the research found "can't get out of filters without applying them." Clear-all is always visible — users can reset without hunting. It only appears when filters are active, so it doesn't clutter the default state.
    06 — Solution
    The redesigned shopping experience

    Warm white background (#FAF8F5), charcoal text (#161616). Amazon orange (#FF9900) appears only in interactive elements — sale badges, Prime badges, Add to Cart button. The page chrome stays neutral so product imagery carries the color.

    Product list: sort dropdown at top (SEPARATE from filters), filter sidebar with category, price range, Prime/stock toggles, and Clear-all button.
    Sort is a dropdown at the top. Filters are in the sidebar. Different controls for different actions.
    Product detail: tabbed interface (Overview / Specs / Reviews) instead of infinite scroll. Each tab is short and scannable.
    Tabs replace infinite scroll. Users jump to specs or reviews without scrolling past everything else.
    Specs tab: structured table with alternating row colors for scannability.
    Mobile: category pills replace sidebar. Sort dropdown stays at top. Clean, not squeezed desktop.
    07 — Outcome
    What changed

    Design study, not a shipped product — so no real metrics. But each fix maps directly to a research finding:

  • Sort separated: Dropdown at top → fixes "strange to see sorting inside filters"
  • Price range inputs: Min/max fields → fixes "can't choose the price range"
  • Clear-all button: Always visible when filters active → fixes "can't get out without applying"
  • Tabbed product detail: Overview / Specs / Reviews → fixes "very long scrolling"
  • Accurate result count: Derived from filtered list → fixes "where did the other 1,600 go?"
    • 08 — Reflection
    What I'd change

    The filter sidebar needs a mobile drawer pattern — currently it's hidden on mobile and replaced with category pills, but price range and Prime/stock toggles aren't accessible on mobile. I'd also add a "Compare" feature — the research showed users struggle to compare products because the product page is so long they can't keep multiple tabs open mentally. Finally, the review filtering could use the same sort-separate-from-filter pattern: sort reviews by helpfulness vs. filter by star rating.
